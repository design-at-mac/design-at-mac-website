Writer pathway

Five stages of a focused reflection

Start with a course or learning context that you know well. Keep the inquiry bounded enough to examine evidence critically and identify a credible next iteration.

1

Identify

Define a precise student-learning problem or opportunity.

2

Gather

Use student perspectives, work, artifacts, observations, and peers.

3

Interpret

Use scholarship to compare explanations and examine assumptions.

4

Act

Explain the intervention and its connection to teaching values.

5

Iterate

Analyze consequences and state the next question and evidence plan.

Reflection structure

A practical writing template

The structure below preserves the red thread without making every reflection look identical.

Context and learning problem

  • Course, students, discipline, and educator role
  • What students struggle to understand or do
  • How the problem became visible

Interpretation and rationale

  • Possible explanations and uncertainties
  • Relevant scholarship
  • Connection to teaching philosophy

Action and evidence

  • What changed and who was involved
  • Student perspectives and work
  • Peer, artifact, and observation evidence

Consequences and next iteration

  • Intended and unintended outcomes
  • Evidence limits and alternative explanations
  • What will be retained, changed, or investigated
McMaster integration

Use reflection with SPS B2

A concise account can be referenced in Part A and included in full, with evidence, in Part B. Always use the current University Secretariat policy as the authority.

Part A

Introduce the context, learning problem, response, principal evidence, and significance.

Part B

Provide the full reflection, scholarly rationale, evidence, artifacts, limitations, and next-iteration plan.
